Research notes

Machine learning for fluid dynamics

From projection-based reduced-order models to latent dynamics networks, neural operators and generative surrogates: the main ideas, the key papers, and open problems.

Living document, first draft (November 2026). Summaries are written from the papers' main ideas; details marked to verify still need checking against the originals. Demo notebooks will be added section by section.

Chapter 1

1. Overview

This page is a working map of data-driven and machine-learning methods for fluid dynamics and, more generally, for parametrized partial differential equations. It is organized like a set of lecture notes: first the problem and a vocabulary, then the classical reduced-order modeling methods that the neural approaches build on, then the neural methods grouped by what they learn. Each method section gives the core idea, the key equation, where it shines and where it breaks, and the papers to read. A small Jupyter notebook demo is planned for each method.

1.1 The many-query problem

Many tasks in engineering and science require solving the same PDE many times: design optimization, uncertainty quantification, control, inverse problems and real-time digital twins. A typical setting is a parametrized, time-dependent problem \[ \partial_t u + \mathcal{N}(u;\mu) = f(t), \qquad x\in\Omega(\mu),\ t\in(0,T], \] where \(\mu\) collects physical or geometric parameters and \(f(t)\) is a time-dependent input such as an inflow profile or an actuation signal. After discretization (finite elements, finite volumes) the state has \(\mathcal{N}_h\) degrees of freedom, often \(10^5\) to \(10^8\). A surrogate replaces the full solver with something much cheaper to evaluate, trained or built from a limited number of full solves.

1.2 What gets learned

Most methods on this page approximate one of four objects. Asking which one a method learns is the fastest way to see what it can and cannot generalize over.

ObjectMapExamples
Parameter-to-solution map\((\mu,t)\mapsto u_h(t;\mu)\) on a fixed meshPOD-NN, DL-ROM, POD-DL-ROM
Operator between function spaces\(a(\cdot)\mapsto u(\cdot)\)DeepONet, FNO, CNO, GINO, Transolver
Time-stepper (flow map)\(u(t)\mapsto u(t+\Delta t)\)MeshGraphNets, MP-PDE, PDE-Refiner
Reduced (latent) dynamics\(\dot z = F(z, f(t))\), then decodeGalerkin ROMs, LDNet, Koopman AE, LaSDI

Generative models learn a distribution over one of these instead of a single prediction. Physics-informed training is not a separate object: it is a loss term that can be added to almost any of the architectures.

1.3 Two research traditions

The literature comes from two communities with different defaults, and much confusion comes from comparing results across them.

  • Numerical analysis and reduced-order modeling (e.g. the groups of Quarteroni, Rozza, Hesthaven, Manzoni, Willcox, Carlberg): few high-fidelity solves, fixed or parametrized geometry, offline/online splitting, emphasis on stability and error control. Benchmarks are specific engineering problems such as cavity flow, cylinder wakes or cardiac electrophysiology.
  • Machine learning and neural operators (e.g. the groups of Anandkumar, Karniadakis, Mishra, Welling, Thuerey): large training sets, generic benchmark suites, emphasis on resolution independence, geometry variation and scaling, and lately foundation models.

Latent Dynamics Networks (Section 3.7) sit deliberately between the two, which is one reason they are a good lens for this page.

1.4 How to read this page

Chapter 2 covers the classical methods that every later chapter refers back to. Chapters 3 to 7 cover neural methods by family. Chapter 8 collects benchmarks and methodological critiques, and Chapter 9 lists open problems. Equations are schematic: notation is unified across sections and may differ from the original papers. Sections marked to verify contain details that still need to be checked against the paper.

Chapter 2

2. Fundamental methods

Projection-based reduced-order models are the baseline every learned surrogate should be compared against when the mesh is fixed. They also supply the vocabulary used later: snapshots, reduced bases, offline/online phases, intrusive vs non-intrusive, and the Kolmogorov n-width.

2.1 Proper orthogonal decomposition and Galerkin projection

Collect snapshots \(S=[u_h(t_1;\mu_1),\dots,u_h(t_m;\mu_m)]\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{N}_h\times m}\) and compute the SVD \(S=U\Sigma W^\top\). The first \(N\) left singular vectors form the POD basis \(V_N\), which is optimal in the least-squares sense among all \(N\)-dimensional linear subspaces for the given snapshots. The solution is approximated as \(u_h\approx V_N u_N\), and the reduced equations come from projecting the full-order residual onto the basis: \[ V_N^\top\big( M V_N \dot u_N + \mathcal{N}_h(V_N u_N;\mu) - f_h(t)\big)=0. \] For nonlinear terms, the projected operator still costs \(O(\mathcal{N}_h)\) to evaluate unless one uses hyper-reduction (empirical interpolation, DEIM) to sample the nonlinearity at a few points.

Strengths: simple, interpretable, the reduced model inherits structure from the discretization. Limitations: intrusive (needs the solver's operators); POD-Galerkin can be unstable for convection-dominated and turbulent flows; and a linear subspace needs many modes when features are transported (the Kolmogorov n-width barrier). This last limitation is the main motivation for the nonlinear methods in Chapter 3.

Papers
  • J. S. Hesthaven, G. Rozza, B. Stamm, Certified Reduced Basis Methods for Parametrized Partial Differential Equations, SpringerBriefs in Mathematics, 2016.

2.2 Reduced basis methods and certified error bounds

The reduced basis method builds the basis from solutions at carefully chosen parameter values. A greedy algorithm repeatedly adds the snapshot at the parameter where an a posteriori error estimator is largest. For coercive problems with an affine parameter dependence the estimator is rigorous: \[ \|u_h(\mu)-V_N u_N(\mu)\|_X \le \frac{\|r_h(V_N u_N(\mu);\mu)\|_{X'}}{\alpha_{\mathrm{LB}}(\mu)}, \] where \(r_h\) is the full-order residual and \(\alpha_{\mathrm{LB}}\) a lower bound of the coercivity (or inf-sup) constant. Thanks to an offline/online decomposition, both the reduced solution and its error bound cost nothing that depends on \(\mathcal{N}_h\) online. Geometric parameters are handled by mapping to a reference domain.

This certification is something almost no neural surrogate offers, and it is a recurring theme in Chapter 9.

Papers
  • J. S. Hesthaven, G. Rozza, B. Stamm, Certified Reduced Basis Methods for Parametrized Partial Differential Equations, SpringerBriefs in Mathematics, 2016.
Demo notebook · planned

Thermal-block or parametrized Stokes problem: greedy basis construction and the effectivity of the error bound (e.g. with pyMOR).

2.3 Operator inference

Operator inference keeps the structure of a projection ROM but learns the reduced operators from data instead of projecting the solver. If the governing equations have polynomial nonlinearities (e.g. quadratic advection), the reduced model must have the form \[ \dot{\hat u} = \hat A\hat u + \hat H(\hat u\otimes\hat u) + \hat B f(t), \] with \(\hat u = V_N^\top u_h\). The matrices \(\hat A,\hat H,\hat B\) are fitted by a (regularized) least-squares problem on projected snapshots and their time derivatives. It is non-intrusive, cheap to train, handles time-dependent inputs naturally, and remains interpretable. Its limits are those of the linear subspace plus the assumption of known polynomial structure.

Papers
  • B. Peherstorfer, K. Willcox, Data-driven operator inference for nonintrusive projection-based model reduction, Comput. Methods Appl. Mech. Eng., 2016.
Demo notebook · planned

Operator inference for 1D Burgers (quadratic nonlinearity): compare with intrusive POD-Galerkin.

2.4 Sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics (SINDy)

SINDy discovers governing equations by sparse regression. Given state measurements \(X\) and derivatives \(\dot X\), build a library \(\Theta(X)\) of candidate terms (polynomials, trigonometric functions, ...) and solve \[ \dot X \approx \Theta(X)\,\Xi, \qquad \Xi \text{ sparse}, \] with sequentially thresholded least squares or another sparsity-promoting method. The result is a short, readable ODE. SINDy was introduced for low-dimensional systems; for fluids it is applied to POD coefficients or to learned latent coordinates (see LaSDI, Section 3.5).

Papers
  • S. L. Brunton, J. L. Proctor, J. N. Kutz, Discovering governing equations from data by sparse identification of nonlinear dynamical systems, PNAS, 2016.
Demo notebook · planned

Recover the Lorenz system, then apply SINDy to POD coefficients of the cylinder wake.

2.5 POD with neural network regression (POD-NN)

The bridge from classical to learned ROMs. Compute a POD basis, then train a neural network to map parameters directly to the reduced coefficients: \[ u_h(\mu) \approx V_N\,\phi_\theta(\mu). \] No equations are projected, so the method is fully non-intrusive and the online cost is one forward pass. Time can be included as an extra input. It inherits the n-width limitation of the linear basis and has no notion of dynamics, so it cannot respond to new time-dependent inputs. Structurally it is a DeepONet (Section 4.1) whose trunk network has been replaced by POD modes.

Papers
  • J. S. Hesthaven, S. Ubbiali, Non-intrusive reduced order modeling of nonlinear problems using neural networks, J. Comput. Phys., 2018.
Demo notebook · planned

POD-NN on a parametrized steady problem (e.g. lid-driven cavity at varying Reynolds number).

Chapter 3

3. Neural reduced-order models and latent dynamics

These methods keep the reduced-order modeling idea of a small latent state but replace the linear basis, the reduced dynamics, or both, with neural networks.

3.1 Nonlinear-manifold ROMs with autoencoders

Replace the linear approximation \(u_h\approx V_N u_N\) with a nonlinear decoder \(u_h\approx g_\theta(z)\), \(z\in\mathbb{R}^n\), learned by a convolutional autoencoder from snapshots. A nonlinear manifold of small dimension can represent transported features that would need many POD modes. The dynamics are still obtained from the governing equations, by projecting the full-order residual onto the tangent space of the manifold (manifold Galerkin) or by minimizing the residual (manifold LSPG): \[ z^{n+1} = \arg\min_z \big\| r_h\big(g_\theta(z);\mu\big)\big\|. \] This is intrusive and needs hyper-reduction to be fast, but it keeps the physics in the time stepping.

Papers
  • K. Lee, K. T. Carlberg, Model reduction of dynamical systems on nonlinear manifolds using deep convolutional autoencoders, J. Comput. Phys., 2020.
Demo notebook · planned

Autoencoder vs POD reconstruction error on an advecting pulse (1D linear advection or Burgers).

3.2 DL-ROM and POD-DL-ROM

DL-ROM, from the Politecnico di Milano group, makes the nonlinear-manifold idea fully non-intrusive. A decoder \(D\) maps a latent code to the full field, an encoder \(E\) maps snapshots to the latent space (used in training only), and a feed-forward network \(\phi\) maps \((t,\mu)\) to the latent code. Schematically the loss is \[ \omega\,\big\|u_h(t;\mu)-D(\phi(t,\mu))\big\|^2 + (1-\omega)\,\big\|E(u_h(t;\mu))-\phi(t,\mu)\big\|^2. \] Online, the model is just \(D\circ\phi\). POD-DL-ROM first compresses the snapshots with (randomized) POD and runs the autoencoder on the POD coefficients, which makes training much cheaper and allows larger problems.

Limitation: time enters as an input coordinate, so there are no actual dynamics and the model cannot respond to a new time-dependent input signal. This is precisely what LDNets address.

Papers
  • S. Fresca, L. Dede', A. Manzoni, A comprehensive deep learning-based approach to reduced order modeling of nonlinear time-dependent parametrized PDEs, J. Sci. Comput., 2021.
  • S. Fresca, A. Manzoni, POD-DL-ROM: enhancing deep learning-based reduced order models for nonlinear parametrized PDEs by proper orthogonal decomposition, Comput. Methods Appl. Mech. Eng., 2022.
Demo notebook · planned

POD-DL-ROM on a parametrized unsteady flow (cylinder wake at varying Reynolds number).

3.3 Graph convolutional autoencoder ROMs (GCA-ROM)

Convolutional autoencoders need structured grids. GCA-ROM uses graph convolutions with mesh-based pooling so the encoder and decoder operate directly on unstructured finite element meshes, with a parameter-to-latent network as in DL-ROM. It brings nonlinear-manifold ROMs to the meshes used in practice and connects the ROM community to the graph-network methods of Chapter 5. The mesh is fixed across samples in the main setting. Which test cases are covered, and whether time dependence is treated, is to verify.

Papers
  • F. Pichi, B. Moya, J. S. Hesthaven, A graph convolutional autoencoder approach to model order reduction for parametrized PDEs, J. Comput. Phys., 2024.
Demo notebook · planned

Graph autoencoder on an unstructured FE mesh (e.g. flow around an obstacle with varying inflow).

3.4 Koopman autoencoders

Koopman theory says that nonlinear dynamics act linearly on a (generally infinite-dimensional) space of observables. A Koopman autoencoder learns a finite set of observables \(z=\varphi(u)\) in which the dynamics are approximately linear, \[ z_{k+1} = K z_k,\qquad u_k \approx \varphi^{-1}(z_k), \] trained with reconstruction, linear-prediction and multi-step losses. Linear latent dynamics are easy to analyze and to use for control and spectral analysis. Systems with continuous spectra and chaotic flows are hard to capture with a fixed linear operator; the paper addresses this partly with an auxiliary network that makes the eigenvalues depend on the state.

Papers
  • B. Lusch, J. N. Kutz, S. L. Brunton, Deep learning for universal linear embeddings of nonlinear dynamics, Nature Communications, 2018.
Demo notebook · planned

Koopman autoencoder for the nonlinear pendulum and for cylinder-wake POD coefficients; compare to DMD.

3.5 Latent space dynamics identification (LaSDI)

LaSDI combines an autoencoder with SINDy: snapshots are encoded to a latent trajectory \(z(t)\), and a sparse, interpretable ODE \(\dot z = \Theta(z)\,\Xi(\mu)\) is identified in the latent space for each training parameter. For a new parameter, the coefficients \(\Xi(\mu)\) are interpolated. The result is an interpretable latent model with genuine dynamics. Its accuracy depends on how well coefficient interpolation works across the parameter space.

Papers
  • W. D. Fries, X. He, Y. Choi, LaSDI: Parametric latent space dynamics identification, Comput. Methods Appl. Mech. Eng., 2022.
Demo notebook · planned

Autoencoder + SINDy on parametrized 1D Burgers; interpolation of coefficients to unseen parameters.

3.6 Learning effective dynamics (LED)

LED uses an autoencoder to obtain a latent state and a recurrent network (LSTM) to advance it in time. Its distinctive idea is multiscale coupling: the model alternates between long, cheap stretches of latent propagation and short bursts of the full solver that bring the trajectory back to the true dynamics. This trades accuracy against cost explicitly and is an early example of the hybrid approaches in Section 5.3.

Papers
  • P. R. Vlachas, G. Arampatzis, C. Uhler, P. Koumoutsakos, Multiscale simulations of complex systems by learning their effective dynamics, Nature Machine Intelligence, 2022.
Demo notebook · planned

Latent RNN on Kuramoto–Sivashinsky with and without periodic full-solver corrections.

3.7 Latent Dynamics Networks (LDNets)

LDNets learn a low-dimensional latent state \(s(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N_s}\) whose evolution is driven by time-dependent inputs \(f(t)\), together with a decoder that returns the field at any query point: \[ \begin{aligned} \dot s(t) &= \mathcal{F}_\theta\big(s(t), f(t)\big),\quad s(0)=s_0,\\ u(x,t)&\approx \mathcal{R}_\theta\big(s(t), x\big). \end{aligned} \] Both \(\mathcal{F}_\theta\) (the dynamics network) and \(\mathcal{R}_\theta\) (the reconstruction network) are small fully connected networks, trained end to end by integrating the latent ODE and matching outputs at observed space–time points.

The key difference from Sections 3.1 to 3.6 is that there is no encoder. The latent state is never obtained by compressing a high-dimensional snapshot; it is generated by the learned dynamics from a fixed starting point, and the model discovers whatever latent coordinates best explain the outputs. Consequences:

  • Training data need not live on a common mesh, and outputs can be queried anywhere in the domain.
  • Time-dependent inputs are first-class, which suits control and digital-twin settings.
  • The models are small, and the latent dimension can be very low.

Limitations and open points: the geometry is fixed; without an encoder, a new observed state (a different initial condition or measurements) cannot be absorbed directly; training backpropagates through the time integration; and there is no built-in physics or error estimate. The paper's test cases include advection–diffusion–reaction, cardiac electrophysiology and an unsteady incompressible flow with time-dependent inputs; the exact configurations are to verify.

Related: DINo (Section 4.6) also combines a latent ODE with a neural-field decoder, and is the closest analogue from the machine-learning community. The reconstruction network \(\mathcal{R}_\theta(s,x)\) has the same "code plus query point" structure as a DeepONet (Section 4.1), with the code coming from a dynamical system instead of a branch network.

Papers
  • F. Regazzoni, S. Pagani, M. Salvador, L. Dede', A. Quarteroni, Learning the intrinsic dynamics of spatio-temporal processes through Latent Dynamics Networks, Nature Communications, 2024.
Demo notebook · planned

Minimal LDNet in JAX or PyTorch on 1D advection–diffusion driven by a time-varying source; compare to POD-DL-ROM on the same data.

Chapter 4

4. Operator learning

Neural operators learn maps between function spaces, for example from a coefficient field or an initial condition to the solution. They are usually trained on many input–output pairs and aim to be independent of the discretization.

4.1 DeepONet

Motivated by a universal approximation theorem for operators, DeepONet writes the output function as a linear combination of learned basis functions of the query point: \[ \mathcal{G}(a)(y) \approx \sum_{k=1}^{p} b_k(a)\,t_k(y) + b_0. \] The branch network takes the input function \(a\) sampled at fixed sensor locations and outputs the coefficients \(b_k\); the trunk network takes the query location \(y\) (space, and possibly time) and outputs the basis functions \(t_k\). Outputs can be queried anywhere, but inputs must be given at the same sensors. Because the output is linear in the trunk basis, DeepONet behaves like a learned linear reduced basis in the output space and faces the same n-width difficulty for transport-dominated problems.

Papers
  • L. Lu, P. Jin, G. Pang, Z. Zhang, G. E. Karniadakis, Learning nonlinear operators via DeepONet based on the universal approximation theorem of operators, Nature Machine Intelligence, 2021.
Demo notebook · planned

DeepONet for the antiderivative operator and for 1D diffusion–reaction; visualize trunk basis functions next to POD modes.

4.2 Fourier Neural Operator and the neural operator framework

A neural operator stacks layers of the form \[ v_{l+1}(x) = \sigma\Big(W v_l(x) + \int_\Omega \kappa_\phi(x,y)\,v_l(y)\,dy\Big). \] The Fourier Neural Operator parametrizes the kernel integral in Fourier space: transform, multiply the lowest \(k_{\max}\) modes by learned weights, transform back, \[ v_{l+1} = \sigma\big(W v_l + \mathcal{F}^{-1}(R_\phi\cdot \mathcal{F}v_l)\big). \] With the FFT each layer costs \(O(N\log N)\) on a uniform grid. Since the weights live on Fourier modes, the same model can be evaluated on different grids; how well this "zero-shot super-resolution" works in practice depends on how much high-frequency content the solution has. The general framework, universal approximation results and other kernel parametrizations (graph, low-rank, multipole) are in the JMLR paper.

Limitations: uniform grids; spectral truncation struggles with shocks and sharp fronts; aliasing when changing resolution (see Section 4.4); autoregressive rollouts accumulate error.

Papers
  • Z. Li, N. Kovachki, K. Azizzadenesheli, B. Liu, K. Bhattacharya, A. Stuart, A. Anandkumar, Fourier Neural Operator for parametric partial differential equations, ICLR, 2021.
  • N. Kovachki, Z. Li, B. Liu, K. Azizzadenesheli, K. Bhattacharya, A. Stuart, A. Anandkumar, Neural Operator: Learning maps between function spaces, JMLR, 2023.
Demo notebook · planned

FNO on 1D Burgers and 2D Navier–Stokes vorticity on the torus; test on a finer grid than training.

4.3 Neural operators on general geometries: Geo-FNO and GINO

FNO needs a regular grid. Geo-FNO learns (or is given) a deformation from the irregular physical domain to a latent uniform grid and applies FNO there. GINO encodes a point cloud and a signed distance function of the geometry onto a regular latent grid with a graph neural operator, applies FNO on that grid, and decodes to arbitrary query points with another graph operator. GINO targets large 3D problems such as surface pressure on car geometries for drag prediction. Both focus mainly on steady problems; unsteady flows with varying geometry remain less explored.

Papers
  • Z. Li, D. Z. Huang, B. Liu, A. Anandkumar, Fourier Neural Operator with learned deformations for PDEs on general geometries, JMLR, 2023.
  • Z. Li et al., Geometry-Informed Neural Operator for large-scale 3D PDEs, NeurIPS, 2023.
Demo notebook · planned

Geo-FNO on flow around airfoils of varying shape (steady).

4.4 Convolutional Neural Operators (CNO)

CNO builds an operator from U-Net-style convolutions while respecting the equivalence between the continuous operator and its discrete implementation: functions are treated as band-limited, and up-sampling, down-sampling and nonlinearities are arranged to avoid aliasing. The paper argues that this makes the learned map a well-defined operator across resolutions and reports gains over FNO and other baselines on a suite of benchmark PDEs. It also reminds us that a well-designed convolutional network is a strong baseline for operator learning on regular grids.

Papers
  • B. Raonić, R. Molinaro, T. De Ryck, T. Rohner, F. Bartolucci, R. Alaifari, S. Mishra, E. de Bézenac, Convolutional Neural Operators for robust and accurate learning of PDEs, NeurIPS, 2023.
Demo notebook · planned

Aliasing experiment: FNO vs CNO evaluated at several resolutions.

4.5 Transformer operators: Galerkin Transformer, GNOT, Transolver

Treat every mesh point as a token and read attention as a learned kernel integral. The Galerkin Transformer drops the softmax and normalizes keys and values instead, giving linear cost in the number of points and an interpretation as a Petrov–Galerkin projection: \[ z = Q\,\frac{K^\top V}{n}. \] GNOT mixes several input functions (geometry, boundary conditions, parameters) through a normalized cross-attention and a geometry-gated mixture of experts. Transolver introduces "physics attention": each point is softly assigned to one of \(M\ll n\) learned slices, attention runs among the \(M\) slice tokens, and results are broadcast back to the points, \[ w = \operatorname{softmax}(\mathrm{Proj}(x)),\qquad z_j = \frac{\sum_i w_{ij}x_i}{\sum_i w_{ij}}. \] These methods handle unstructured meshes and varying geometry. The slice tokens are effectively a learned low-dimensional representation, which suggests an interesting link to reduced bases that has not been explored much.

Papers
  • S. Cao, Choose a Transformer: Fourier or Galerkin, NeurIPS, 2021.
  • Z. Hao et al., GNOT: A General Neural Operator Transformer for operator learning, ICML, 2023.
  • H. Wu, H. Luo, H. Wang, J. Wang, M. Long, Transolver: A fast Transformer solver for PDEs on general geometries, ICML, 2024.
Demo notebook · planned

Small Transolver on an airfoil or elasticity dataset; visualize the learned slices next to POD modes.

4.6 Neural-field operators: DINo and CORAL

Represent each field by a small code \(z\) that modulates a shared implicit neural representation \(f_\theta(x; z)\), then learn the dynamics or the operator on the codes. DINo obtains codes by auto-decoding (optimizing a code per sample) and integrates a latent ODE on them, giving a model that is continuous in space and time and works from irregularly sampled observations. CORAL uses the same idea with meta-learned codes and learns operators between codes, handling general geometries. These are the ML-community relatives of LDNets: the main differences are that LDNets do not fit codes to observed states and that their latent dynamics are driven by external inputs.

Papers
  • Y. Yin, M. Kirchmeyer, J.-Y. Franceschi, A. Rakotomamonjy, P. Gallinari, Continuous PDE dynamics forecasting with implicit neural representations, ICLR, 2023.
  • L. Serrano et al., Operator learning with neural fields: Tackling PDEs on general geometries, NeurIPS, 2023.
Demo notebook · planned

Fit a modulated neural field to 2D vorticity snapshots and learn a latent ODE on the codes.

Chapter 5

5. Mesh-based learned simulators and hybrid solvers

Instead of learning a global map, these methods learn a local update rule on the simulation mesh and apply it repeatedly, or they keep a numerical solver and learn a correction to it.

5.1 MeshGraphNets

MeshGraphNets learn the time step \(u(t)\mapsto u(t+\Delta t)\) with message passing on the simulation mesh in an encode–process–decode architecture. Each processing layer updates edges and then nodes, \[ e'_{ij} = f_e(e_{ij}, v_i, v_j), \qquad v'_i = f_v\Big(v_i, \sum_j e'_{ij}\Big). \] Training uses one-step targets with noise injection so that long rollouts stay stable, and the method supports adaptive remeshing. It handles unstructured meshes and varying geometry naturally. The limitations are error accumulation over long rollouts and the limited receptive field of local message passing: effects that are global, such as pressure in incompressible flow, need many layers or multiscale graphs.

Papers
  • T. Pfaff, M. Fortunato, A. Sanchez-Gonzalez, P. W. Battaglia, Learning mesh-based simulation with graph networks, ICLR, 2021.
Demo notebook · planned

Tiny MeshGraphNet on a 2D cylinder-flow dataset; rollout error vs time.

5.2 Message passing neural PDE solvers

This work studies what it takes to make message-passing solvers stable and general: the pushforward trick (training on the model's own perturbed predictions to reduce distribution shift in rollouts), temporal bundling (predicting several future steps at once), and conditioning on PDE parameters so that one model covers a family of equations.

Papers
  • J. Brandstetter, D. Worrall, M. Welling, Message passing neural PDE solvers, ICLR, 2022.
Demo notebook · planned

Effect of temporal bundling and the pushforward trick on rollout stability for 1D Burgers/KdV.

5.3 Learned corrections inside a numerical solver

Keep a differentiable CFD solver at coarse resolution and learn the parts it gets wrong. In the paper by Kochkov and coauthors, a convolutional network supplies learned interpolation coefficients inside a finite-volume scheme for 2D turbulence, trained end to end by unrolling the solver so that coarse simulations track downsampled high-resolution ones. Because the solver's structure (conservation, time stepping) is kept, these models tend to be more stable and generalize better than pure surrogates. They require a differentiable solver, and the paper treats periodic 2D domains. The analogous idea in reduced-order modeling is closure modeling for POD-Galerkin ROMs.

Papers
  • D. Kochkov, J. A. Smith, A. Alieva, Q. Wang, M. P. Brenner, S. Hoyer, Machine learning–accelerated computational fluid dynamics, PNAS, 2021.
Demo notebook · planned

Learned correction for a coarse 1D Burgers finite-volume solver, trained by unrolling in JAX.

Chapter 6

6. Physics-informed learning

Physics-informed methods put the governing equations into the loss function. They can be combined with most architectures above.

6.1 Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs)

Represent the solution by a network \(u_\theta(x,t)\) and minimize the PDE residual at collocation points together with boundary, initial and data misfits: \[ \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \frac{1}{N_r}\sum_{r}\big|\partial_t u_\theta + \mathcal{N}(u_\theta)\big|^2(x_r,t_r) + \lambda_b\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BC/IC}} + \lambda_d\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{data}}. \] Derivatives are computed by automatic differentiation, so no mesh is needed. A plain PINN solves one problem instance; training is the solve, and for forward problems it is usually far slower than a classical solver at the same accuracy. Optimization is delicate (imbalanced loss terms, spectral bias, violated causality in time), and high-Reynolds-number flows are hard. The strongest use case is inverse problems and data assimilation, where sparse measurements and the equations are combined, for example to infer pressure or viscosity from velocity data.

Papers
  • M. Raissi, P. Perdikaris, G. E. Karniadakis, Physics-informed neural networks: A deep learning framework for solving forward and inverse problems involving nonlinear partial differential equations, J. Comput. Phys., 2019.
Demo notebook · planned

PINN for 1D Burgers (forward) and for identifying the viscosity from sparse data (inverse).

6.2 Physics-informed operator learning

Adding the residual loss to an operator network turns it into a parametric solver: physics-informed DeepONets can be trained with few or no solution data, using the PDE residual at collocation points for many input functions. The same idea applied to FNO gives physics-informed neural operators. The residual term acts as a regularizer that reduces the amount of simulation data needed, at the price of the optimization difficulties of PINNs.

Papers
  • S. Wang, H. Wang, P. Perdikaris, Learning the solution operator of parametric partial differential equations with physics-informed DeepONets, Science Advances, 2021.

Physics-informed DeepONet for a 1D diffusion–reaction operator trained without solution data.

Chapter 7

7. Generative models and foundation models

Recent work uses diffusion models to capture uncertainty and small scales, and large pretrained models to transfer across PDEs.

7.1 PDE-Refiner

Neural time-steppers trained with a mean-squared-error loss tend to neglect low-amplitude, high-frequency components of the solution, and these errors grow during long rollouts. PDE-Refiner follows each one-step prediction with a few denoising-style refinement steps that progressively recover those components, which substantially improves long-rollout accuracy and stability on chaotic test problems.

Papers
  • P. Lippe, B. S. Veeling, P. Perdikaris, R. E. Turner, J. Brandstetter, PDE-Refiner: Achieving accurate long rollouts with neural PDE solvers, NeurIPS, 2023.
Demo notebook · planned

Spectrum of one-step errors with and without refinement on Kuramoto–Sivashinsky.

7.2 Autoregressive conditional diffusion for turbulent flows

Instead of predicting the next state, learn its conditional distribution \(p(u_{k+1}\mid u_k,\mu)\) with a diffusion model and sample autoregressively. This work benchmarks such models against deterministic surrogates on several flow problems, including turbulent cases, and finds better long-horizon behaviour and physically meaningful sample spread, at a much higher cost per step because each step requires many denoising iterations.

Papers
  • G. Kohl, L.-W. Chen, N. Thuerey, Benchmarking autoregressive conditional diffusion models for turbulent flow simulation, arXiv, 2023.
Demo notebook · planned

Conditional diffusion vs deterministic U-Net for a 2D wake: ensemble spread and long-time statistics.

7.3 Diffusion-based ensemble forecasting at scale

GenCast applies conditional diffusion to global medium-range weather forecasting and produces probabilistic ensembles that compete with operational ensemble systems. It shows that generative surrogates can work at very large scale for a chaotic fluid system, given enough data (decades of reanalysis).

Papers
  • I. Price et al., GenCast: Diffusion-based ensemble forecasting for medium-range weather, arXiv, 2023.

7.4 PDE foundation models: Poseidon

Poseidon pretrains a multiscale operator transformer on a large and diverse set of 2D fluid-flow trajectories and then fine-tunes it on downstream PDEs with few samples. Time enters as a lead-time input, and training uses all pairs of time steps in each trajectory to get more out of the data. The paper reports strong sample efficiency on downstream tasks, including some physics not present in pretraining. Open questions are transfer to 3D, to complex geometries, and to the small-data engineering problems typical of reduced-order modeling.

Papers
  • M. Herde, B. Raonić, T. Rohner, R. Käppeli, R. Molinaro, E. de Bézenac, S. Mishra, Poseidon: Efficient foundation models for PDEs, NeurIPS, 2024.

Chapter 8

8. Benchmarks and methodology

How methods are compared matters as much as the methods themselves.

8.1 Benchmark suites

PDEBench collects many 1D, 2D and 3D problems, including advection, Burgers, diffusion–reaction, Darcy flow, shallow water and compressible and incompressible Navier–Stokes, with baseline models and forward and inverse tasks. Shared suites made comparisons easier, but they are mostly initial-value or steady problems on simple domains. ROM-style settings with time-dependent inputs, few training simulations and complex geometry are under-represented. More recent fluid-specific benchmarks (FlowBench, CFDBench and others) are listed for a later update.

Papers
  • M. Takamoto et al., PDEBench: An extensive benchmark for scientific machine learning, NeurIPS Datasets and Benchmarks, 2022.

8.2 Weak baselines and reporting bias

McGreivy and Hakim reviewed machine-learning papers on fluid-related PDEs and found that many reported speedups compare against weak baselines: numerical solvers that are not state of the art, or comparisons not made at equal accuracy. Combined with the tendency to publish only positive results, this leads to overly optimistic conclusions. Their recommendation is to compare against strong solvers at equal accuracy or equal runtime. The same standard should apply when neural ROMs are compared with well-tuned projection ROMs.

Papers
  • N. McGreivy, A. Hakim, Weak baselines and reporting biases lead to overoptimism in machine learning for fluid-related partial differential equations, Nature Machine Intelligence, 2024.

Chapter 9

9. Open problems and research directions

A first list of gaps that stand out when the methods above are put side by side. None of these has yet been checked against the most recent literature.

9.1 Geometry and dynamics are handled by different methods

Methods that handle varying geometry (GINO, GNOT, Transolver, CORAL, MeshGraphNets) are either steady or step the full field autoregressively. Methods with compact latent dynamics (LDNets, LaSDI, Koopman autoencoders, LED) assume a fixed geometry. A natural direction is geometry-conditioned latent dynamics: give an LDNet's reconstruction and dynamics networks a geometry code (e.g. from a signed distance function encoder) and test on unsteady flows past families of shapes under time-varying inflow.

9.2 Data assimilation for encoder-free latent models

LDNets cannot correct their latent state from new measurements. Because the latent state is small, ensemble Kalman or particle filters in latent space, with the reconstruction network as observation operator at sensor locations, should be cheap and could turn LDNets into practical digital twins.

9.3 Error estimates for learned surrogates

Certified reduced basis methods come with a posteriori error bounds; almost no neural surrogate does. When the decoder is differentiable in space (neural fields, LDNets), the PDE residual of the prediction can be computed cheaply and used as an error indicator, possibly calibrated with conformal prediction on held-out solves.

9.4 Uncertainty and chaotic flows

A small deterministic latent ODE cannot represent turbulence, while generative full-field models are expensive. Stochastic or generative dynamics in a compact latent space (latent SDEs, latent diffusion) combined with a neural-field decoder could give cheap ensembles with the right statistics.

9.5 Structure-preserving latent dynamics

Constraining the learned latent dynamics to be stable, dissipative or port-Hamiltonian could make long rollouts under unseen input signals reliable, at some cost in expressiveness for genuinely unstable regimes such as the onset of vortex shedding.

9.6 Fair comparisons across the two traditions

Hardly anyone compares projection ROMs, neural ROMs and neural operators on the same data, with the same budget and against strong solvers. A small, careful benchmark with time-dependent inputs (inflow, actuation, moving boundaries) and classical baselines included would be valuable on its own and would anchor the directions above.
